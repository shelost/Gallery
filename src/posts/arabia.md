Last spring I argued that the United States and China are Rome and Persia: two powers that can hurt each other for decades and cannot finish the job, until someone who was not invited walks off with the century. I named the rivalry. I did not name the someone. That essay is [here](/persia), if you want the wars themselves.

This is the part I sat on, because the someone in my head is Korea, and I am Korean. I have been calling it nationalism slop in my own head for a year, which is usually a sign that I should either throw a thought away or write it down properly. I already wrote, in [another essay](/tribalism), that when a person's ideas and their people point in different directions, the pronoun gives them away. I don't get an exemption because the analogy is older than a flag.

**The shape of the prediction is this. The third power in the Roman-Persian story was not a third superpower waiting on the bench. It was a divided, culturally loud people stuck between the two empires, who mattered only after both of them had spent themselves. That position, in Northeast Asia, is the Korean peninsula. What does not follow is the fantasy that goes with it: Korean armies, or Korean settlers, inheriting Manchuria and the Russian Far East the way the Caliphate inherited Syria and Iraq. The first claim is a map. The second is a wish.**

Let's dive in.


## The Map

A Persian shah once called Rome and Persia the two eyes of the world. If you keep going around the head, the rest of the face has names too. Some of them fit better than others.

**Europe is Etruria.** Before Rome was Rome, northern Italy belonged to a rich federation of city-states that gave the city its alphabet, a good deal of its religion, and the symbols of its public life. Rome outgrew them, absorbed them, and left their old country cultured, prosperous, and no longer the thing that projected power. That is a better story for Europe than Troy. Americans like the Trojan one, because Virgil wrote it and the Mayflower looks enough like a flight from a burning city if you squint. Troy was destroyed. Europe was not. It remained the center of the world until the twentieth century handed the fleet to its offspring. The Etruscans were not destroyed either. They were succeeded, and then they got to be old.

**America is Rome.** Maritime, commercial, legalistic, convinced that its constitution is a moral fact and not just a local habit, and already deep into the argument about whether the republic is still a republic. I said my piece on that in the first essay. The part I would add is that late Rome was also a culture that had been conquered by a culture it had conquered.

**Japan is Greece.** Horace, who was not paying anyone a compliment, wrote that captive Greece took her savage conqueror captive. Educated Romans spoke Greek at home, hired Greek tutors, and collected Greek statues, and men like Cato the Elder spent their careers saying it was making Roman sons soft. The American who cares more about animation, games, and a certain idea of Japanese craft than about his own mass culture is playing that part. *(What the internet calls a weeaboo, Rome would have called a Grecophile, and a senator would have called a symptom.)*

The violent half of the parallel is real and easy to overdraw. Alexander took a fractured Greece and broke the Persian empire in a decade, and the thing he built died with him. Imperial Japan broke what was left of Qing prestige in 1895, occupied the rich eastern half of China from 1931 to 1945, and was finished in the summer of 1945, leaving behind a China that has organized itself ever since around never being that helpless again. Japan did not finish the conquest. Alexander did. The useful resemblance is the shock, the brevity, and the wound.

If you want a Xerxes, take Kublai Khan. Xerxes threw the wealth of a continental empire at a maritime people and lost a fleet to storms and to a naval battle. Kublai, a Mongol using a Chinese empire's machinery, threw two armadas at Japan, in 1274 and 1281, and lost them to the samurai and to the weather. Persia's quarrel with Greece became a quarrel with Rome, because Rome had swallowed Greece and inherited the sea. China's quarrel with Japan became a quarrel with America for the same reason. From Beijing, the alliance looks like one maritime blockade running from the 1890s to now. I think that is the cleanest piece of the whole map, and it does not require me to flatter anyone I'm related to.

**China is Persia.** An old continental civilization, conquered over and over, and in the habit of converting its conquerors. The Xianbei, the Khitans, the Jurchens, the Mongols, and the Manchus all took the throne and came out, in a generation or three, administering China in Chinese. Persia did the same thing to the people who took Iran: Parthians, Seljuks, the Mongol Ilkhanate. You can argue the edges of this forever. The Qing were never only Chinese, and the Seleucids were never really Persian. The pattern is still the pattern. Both places have a core that survives the dynasty. Both places have also had the experience of watching the outside world's name for their whole region change, while they stayed who they were. For a thousand years the high civilization of the Middle East, to an outsider, meant Persia. After the seventh century the default flipped to Arab and Muslim, and Iran went on being Iran, in Persian. Hold onto that. It matters later, and it is the opposite of a conquest fantasy.

**Russia is the steppe, with a caveat.** Attila is the figure people reach for, and parts of him fit. He did not need to take Constantinople. He raided the Balkans until the emperor paid him, and he cultivated the feeling that he might do anything. A war whose point is to extract concessions, break a security arrangement, and live in your enemy's head is an Attila kind of war. What he is not is a prophecy about Moscow. Attila led a confederation at the top of its momentum, and when he died in 453 his sons fought and the whole thing was gone inside a generation. Russia is an old state, nuclear, bureaucratic, and short of young people. Those do not vanish on the night the leader dies.

The other caveat is that Attila was Rome's problem. Persia's steppe problem had a different name. In 484 the Sasanian king Peroz died in battle against the Hephthalites, and the empire paid tribute north for years. The northern frontier has always hit both eyes. It has not always been the same people. A Russia that bleeds itself against the West and ends up the junior partner of China is doing both jobs at once, badly: draining Rome's magazines the way Attila did, and sitting on Persia's flank as a dependent rather than as a conqueror. The dependency is the part that opens a vacuum. The vacuum is in the east, where the population is thin and the patron is busy.


## The Two Buffers

Before anyone had heard of Islam, the Arab border was already split into two client kingdoms.

The Ghassanids camped on the Roman side, around Jabiya, in the shadow of Bostra. They were Christians, fought Rome's eastern wars, took Roman titles, and lived off the Roman payroll. The Lakhmids ruled from al-Hira, down by the Euphrates, inside the Persian sphere. They were the other shield, pointed the other way. The Arabic tradition has both dynasties coming up out of the south, speaking the same language, claiming the same kind of ancestors. They spent the sixth century killing each other anyway, including during stretches when Rome and Persia were officially at peace. Tradition remembers a battle in 554, the Day of Halima, in which the Lakhmid king al-Mundhir was killed. How much of that story is poetry, I couldn't tell you. The arrangement isn't poetry. Two branches of one people, hired by opposite empires, to bleed on the frontier so the empires didn't have to bleed at home.

If you want the modern version, it is not subtle. South Korea is the Ghassanid kingdom: rich, armed by the maritime empire, culturally tangled up with it, and periodically unsure whether the patron still remembers the alliance is mutual. North Korea is the Lakhmid one: a garrison state on the continental empire's flank, dependent on that empire's tolerance, and jealous of its own dynasty anyway. The Korean War was the hot bill for this arrangement, paid in person, while Washington and Moscow declined to fight a war of capitals. China, which in this map is supposed to be Persia, was already in the trenches. The analogy is a structure, not a reincarnation. I would not build a timeline out of it. I would notice that one people has been hired, for seventy years, to face itself.

The patrons were never fully comfortable with the hired guns. The Ghassanids were Miaphysite Christians, which the emperors in Constantinople regarded as a heresy, and in 581 the court arrested its own Arab king and shipped him to Sicily. The alliance was the frontier. The theology was an excuse to distrust the frontier. Anyone who has watched Seoul and Washington spend a decade discovering new reasons to resent each other, while neither of them can actually leave, has seen this mood. It is not the same fact. It is the same mood.

Persia's version ended worse. Around 602, Khosrow II got rid of the last Lakhmid king, al-Nu'man, and put the border under his own men. A few years later, at a place called Dhi Qar, tribes of the Bakr ibn Wa'il beat a Persian force in the open. Arab memory filed this as the first time they had done that. It was a border fight, not a revolution, and nobody at the time thought a new world religion was about to abolish the Persian empire. People told the story anyway. The shah had eaten his buffer to feel safer, and the border got louder.

I am not predicting that Beijing will depose the Kim family and that a unified Korea will then appear, offended, at the Yalu. I am saying that the historical function of a buffer is to be there, and that patrons get tired of buffers at exactly the moment they can least afford to. Khosrow's Persia in 602 is the confident version of the story. The war he started that same year, the one that ran to 628, is the version where confidence goes to die. Both empires "won" it in turns. Four years after the peace, Muhammad was dead and the armies were already moving. Nineteen years after that, the last Sasanian king was murdered near Merv.

That, and not a Korean century written in advance, is the event I think we are living in the shadow of. A cold version of it. Nuclear weapons mean there is no Heraclius marching into Sichuan, and no Shahrbaraz camped across the river from Washington. The 602–628 of this century, if it happens, is sanctions, chip blockades, proxy wars, and two treasuries funding a rivalry that cannot be won by invasion. Procopius says that under Justinian, monks smuggled silkworm eggs through Persia's silk monopoly in a hollow reed, and Constantinople built its own industry. Reshoring is an old idea. So is the part everyone forgets: breaking the monopoly did not make either empire young again.


## The Part That Is Slop

Here is the version I do not believe, stated plainly so I can't sneak it back in later.

A unified Korea, armed with a nationalist story about Goguryeo and Balhae, spills north into a depopulated Russian Far East and a wobbling Manchuria, and the region becomes Korean the way the Middle East became Arab. China remains Chinese, the way Iran remained Persian. The default image of East Asia flips. Then Korea and America face each other across the Pacific the way the Caliphate and Byzantium faced each other across the Mediterranean.

The demography is fatal to the literal reading. South Korea's fertility rate was 0.80 in 2025. It is still the lowest of any country. People who watch these numbers say this year might scrape toward 0.9, which would count as a recovery from 0.72 and would still be a society in demographic retreat. The North is not a reservoir of spare youth either. Northeast China holds close to a hundred million people. The entire Russian Far East holds under eight. A shrinking peninsula does not colonize either of them. Robotics changes what a small workforce can *produce*. It does not put Korean children into someone else's cities. Those are different verbs, and the slop version depends on pretending they are the same.

The military reading is fatal for a duller reason. China and Russia have nuclear weapons. Rome and Persia did not. There is no seventh-century scenario in which a third people annexes the northeast and the two empires file a complaint. There is a scenario in which everyone dies. I am not interested in that scenario, and it isn't a prediction, it's a failure.

The cultural reading is the tempting one, and it still isn't Arabization. Arabization meant the language of law, prayer, and ordinary speech changed, across a huge geography, and stayed changed. K-dramas are not that. People already watch Korean television, play Japanese games, use American software, and go home to speak Chinese or Russian. Consumer culture stacks. Civilizations replace. The Fair of Ukaz, where pre-Islamic Arab tribes competed in poetry while remaining politically negligible, really is a good picture of a culture getting prestigious before it gets powerful. The difference is that the poets and the conquerors turned out to be the same people, in the same language. A global audience singing along to a chorus is not a people on the edge of adopting the language of its government.

And the vacuum, right now, has a tenant. China shares a four-thousand-kilometer border with the Russian Far East. Since 2022 it has been the region's lifeline, because the war closed the European door and South Korean firms, tied to the American sanctions regime, pulled back from a market they had actually been cultivating. Moscow spent the 2010s trying to get Korean capital into Vladivostok on purpose, as a counterweight. Moon Jae-in went to the Eastern Economic Forum in 2017 and talked about a northern policy. Then the war made the counterweight unavailable, and Beijing inherited the opening by default.

There is a Korean history in that region, and it argues against destiny rather than for it. From the 1860s, farmers and exiles built a real community around Vladivostok and Ussuriysk. In 1937 Stalin deported about 170,000 of them to Central Asia. The Koryo-saram are a diaspora now, not a seed waiting in the ground. North Korean contract labor in the Far East is a labor export by a state that needs currency. It is not a folk migration. Anyone who looks at a construction crew and sees the Rashidun is watching a movie.

For the Russian Far East to tilt toward Seoul rather than toward Beijing, several large things have to happen at once. A land corridor through the North, which means reunification or something close enough to it that a train can leave Busan and arrive in Vladivostok. A thaw between Seoul and Moscow deep enough to survive American alliance politics. And a Russia that still wants a counterweight, rather than a Russia that has become too dependent on China to ask for one. Three conditionals is not a fate. It is a door that is currently shut.

There is a further problem, and it cuts the other way. Seventh-century Arabia was outside the Roman-Persian economic machine. It could afford for both empires to fall over, because it was not their supplier. Korea is inside the machine. Memory chips, batteries, the ordinary guts of everyone else's industry. In the silk story, that is not the Arab role. That is the silkworm. And Justinian's monks exist in the story because both empires eventually try to steal the eggs. Taiwan is the more famous egg. Korea is another one. Being indispensable and being sovereign are not the same experience. A cold war of 628 squeezes a supplier before it liberates a bystander.

I also don't think the third power has to be the poetic one. India is younger, bigger, and sitting on the sea lanes, and it does not need a metaphor about the Ghassanids to benefit from a US-China rivalry that wastes both of their attention. If the analogy were a forecast, a sane person would bet on India before betting on a peninsula of fifty million. The analogy is a description of a *position*: the people between the two eyes, already divided into the two eyes' client kingdoms, already famous before they are united. India is not in that position. Korea is. Both sentences can be true. Only one of them is flattering to me, which is why I trust the India sentence more and keep coming back to the other one.


## What I Actually Think

Strip the wish out and something remains. It is smaller, and it is enough to be worth saying.

The unifying mechanism is the part I believe. The Ghassanids and the Lakhmids did not become one people because a committee found their fiscal systems compatible. They became one people because a story showed up that was larger than either patron. North and South Korea are not going to merge because a white paper demonstrates the synergies. If they ever do, the economic and human friction will be enormous, a hyper-capitalist society and a totalitarian one trying to occupy the same state, and a leadership that wants the result to survive will reach for a story big enough to make the friction feel like a price rather than a reason to stop. Historically, that story faces outward. It names an enemy, or a mission, or a lost north. This is the part of the prediction that should worry Koreans first, before it worries anyone else. The Samhan worked as a founding myth because Silla had already won and needed the conquered to have always been family. A myth told beforehand, about land that currently belongs to two nuclear powers, is how buffer states get themselves destroyed.

So the interesting question is what the story is made of, if it isn't irredentism.

The raw materials are already lying around, and they are an odd pile. Korean Protestantism, especially the kind that built the mega-churches, runs on collective fervor, organizational scale, and a sense of being sent. Juche, with the cult torn off it, is a claim that people are the masters of their own condition and that a nation which depends on a patron has already lost. And the technological culture, the hurry, treats speed as a virtue in itself. None of these is Islam. Together they are the kind of material a universalizing ideology gets built from: the scripture of the empire you live inside, plus the self-reliance of the people the empire hired, plus a promise that history is going somewhere specific.

Early Islam did exactly that operation on Judaism and Christianity. It kept the one God, demoted the previous revelations to rough drafts, and gave a fragmented peninsula a reason to be a single political fact. There is a verse revealed when Persia was winning, the surah of the Romans, in which the believers are told that Rome will recover, and that they will be glad. The People of the Book were still kin, as against the fire-worshippers. A Korean synthesis built out of Protestant capitalism would tilt the same way in a US-China conflict. It would recognize America as the civilization it was made from, and treat the Chinese party-state as the other kind of thing. Kin is not the same as obedient. Byzantium was pleased to have Arab buffers right up until the buffers had a caliph.

I am not going to invent the caliph, or the holy sites. Mount Paektu as Mecca and a founder in Pangyo with a manifesto is the sentence where this stops being an argument and starts being scripture for my own tribe. A universal story that only Koreans can believe is just nationalism with a liturgy. The test, if any such thing ever appears, is whether a person who is not Korean can join it without becoming Korean. Islam passed that test, which is why it is still here. Most nationalisms do not, and should not be congratulated for trying.

What technology changes, and this is the only place I think the twenty-first century genuinely breaks the analogy, is the relationship between population and power. Korea already runs the densest automated factories on earth, on the order of a thousand industrial robots for every ten thousand manufacturing workers. If the machines keep getting more general, a country can stay influential while it shrinks. That influence looks like standards, platforms, logistics, and aesthetics that other cities adopt because they work. It looks, if you want a historical picture that isn't the conquest of Persia, like the way Islam actually moved through maritime Southeast Asia: merchants, prestige, and a form of life, not the armies that took Damascus. The Caliphate's armies are the famous part. They are not the only part.

The Pacific sequel, if you insist on playing the tape past 651, is a warning and not a prize. Byzantium and the caliphates spent centuries wearing each other down across the Mediterranean. Neither inherited the sea. The sea went, in time, to Turks coming off the steppe and to Atlantic Europeans who had been peripheral when the great war was fought. A Korea and an America that survived China only to make each other the next defining enemy would be doing the same thing, and the beneficiary would be whoever had spent those decades getting larger. India. Possibly a Southeast Asian bloc that never had to choose a patron. I like my civilization too much to root for that chapter. The surah of the Romans cuts both ways. You can be glad your cousin-empire survived the other eye, and still be the thing that ruins him later.

China, through all of this, stays China. That is the Iran point, and I'll end the prediction on it because it is the one that keeps the rest honest. The center of a civilization that has digested every conqueror for two thousand years does not stop being itself because a peninsula has better software and a more exportable youth culture. What can change is the periphery, and the stereotype. Outsiders once said "the East" and meant Persia. Now they say the Middle East and picture something Arab. Outsiders say East Asia and mean China, and for a while in the late twentieth century they meant Japan. A Korean decade in the aesthetics of the region would be a real event. It would not be a change of ownership. Iran is still there. It is still Persian. The maps around it are what moved.


## Palmyra

One more parallel, and then I'll stop, because this one is about the narrator.

In the third century the most interesting city on the Roman-Persian frontier was Palmyra. Arab rulers, Roman titles, bilingual merchants, a caravan fortune, and a queen, Zenobia, who for a year or two held Egypt and a chunk of Anatolia and looked exactly like a third power. Aurelian put an end to it. The atlas in the first essay gives Palmyra one bright entry and then hands the territory back. The people who actually ended the two-eye world, three and a half centuries later, did not come from Palmyra. They came from further out, from places the Palmyrenes would have thought of as the edge.

I live in New York. In this map that makes me a Palmyrene: fluent in the maritime empire's language, connected to the old country, tempted to believe that the bilingual class is where history is paying attention. It isn't. The bilingual class writes the essays. Someone else, usually poorer and more serious than the essayist, decides whether the story is real.

Do you see the parallels yet?

I think the United States and China cannot decisively beat each other, and that a long, cold, expensive attempt will be the fact of the next few decades. I think the peninsula between them is the place where a new story would have to come from, if a new story comes from inside this particular rivalry rather than from India or from somewhere no one is analogizing. I think that story, if it is any good, will be made of American religion and Korean self-reliance and machines, and will be embarrassing to everyone involved, especially to me. I do not think it conquers Manchuria. I do not think I am qualified to see it coming. Palmyra didn't.

But again: this is all just the rambling of a random guy on the internet. Take it with a grain of salt.
